01

Three shapes, not one

A device reports about twenty-three fields. They do not behave alike. Some are numbers that move constantly and are worth a series. Some are states that hold for hours and are worth recording only when they change. Some were being stored and never read at all.

Storing all three as one snapshot means the cost of the noisiest field is paid by every field. A wireless-charging flip — one bit — cost a whole 641-byte row.

Numbers → device_samples

Battery, temperature, RAM used and total, free storage, Wi-Fi RSSI. Fixed columns, keyed (device_id, at) — the order every reader wants, so it needs no second index, and every insert lands at the right edge instead of dirtying a random page.

States → device_state_events

Charging, pad status, screen, Wi-Fi, IP, timezone, boot. One row per real change, plus one seed row recording a value the first time it is seen. 3,880 rows covered the whole fleet.

Why the columns hold the reading, not a rounded one

The first cut stored tenths of a degree and whole percent. Smaller — and it would have made the CSV export and the chart quote different numbers for one reading: 34.400001525878906 against 34.4, 55.3% against 55%. Two surfaces disagreeing about the same measurement is not a rounding question, it is a correctness one. RAM is kept as used and total so a percentage is computed the same way everywhere.

02

What happens to a check-in now

Every report still updates the live snapshot on devices — that is what the dashboard, the map and the SSE stream read, and it is unaffected by any of this. What changed is everything downstream of it.

The trap in retiring the column

The store-or-skip decision used to compare the incoming report against last.extra — the previous stored row's snapshot. Empty that column and the comparison is false for every report carrying any non-volatile key, so every report stores a row. Retiring the snapshot naively multiplies the write volume it exists to cut, and nothing about it looks broken. Hence state_hash: the row keeps a 32-character digest of exactly the projection that comparison made, and nothing else.

03

Who reads what

Nine readers moved, one at a time, each verified against the old source on real data before the next. Every one keeps a fallback to the stored snapshots for windows the shaped tables do not reach — which is why those rows are kept rather than deleted.

One decision, applied everywhere

Readers were not rewritten against typed columns. Instead each reader is handed a rebuilt extra object assembled from the shaped tables. Every formatting rule the export and the rollup have — an SSID stripped of Android's own quotes, a boolean printed as 0/1, a temperature at one decimal — therefore stays in exactly one place and cannot drift between two sources. For the rollup this goes further: the hundred lines of time-weighted aggregation are one shared constant, so the arithmetic is not merely equivalent between the two paths, it is the same characters.

04

Two bugs the shape exposed

A field nothing read, writing a row every ten seconds

charger_voltage_mv was missing from the volatile-key list, so its last-digit jitter counted as a state change and forced a history row — on a field the server has never read. One line moved rows-forced-by-a-change from 90.3% to 6.7%.

A change log with no starting point

The transition writer recorded a key only when it changed, comparing against a snapshot that already held every value — so no device existing at the time ever got a first event. timezone had 0 events against 150 devices reporting it; charging 44 against 128. A state nobody had a baseline for reads as “never charging”, not as an error.

Why backdating the missing baselines was sound

The writer records every change, so a device with no event for a key has not changed it since the stream began, and its current value is what it held throughout. That premise was tested rather than assumed: across 696 device-and-key pairs that do have events, the latest event's value equals the device's current value in every single case. In the end no repair was needed at all — a device with no baseline is a device that is offline, and an offline device writes no samples; when it returns, its baseline lands in the same transaction as its first sample.

Retention, restated

Retention deletes a period outright. What a fleet actually wants from old history is its shape — when a device was powered, charging, on a pad — and that survives at a fraction of the rows. Five-minute buckets were chosen because the daily rollup weights each sample by the gap to the next one, capped at 600 s: at or under that cap, a thinned day still recomputes its online minutes correctly.

Every figure measured against production or a restored copy of it. Fleet of 150 devices on mdm.dev.aioapp.com. The historical snapshots are being reclaimed in two steps: a housekeeping job strips the keys device_samples already holds — 52% of the remaining bytes, measured on a real day, with no information lost anywhere — and tools/partition-checkins.sql converts the table to monthly partitions, which turns retention into a DROP TABLE and drops checkins_pkey (756 MB, 0 scans) on the way.
