The restaurant report, for people who run restaurants

Today's weekly report is written for us: uptime hours out of 168, "battery drain due to wireless charging" in %/min, serial numbers. The people it goes to are owners, GMs and floor managers, who want to know three things: did the tablets work when we were open, did guests use them, and is there anything I need to do. Six ways to answer that, all drawn from the same made-up week at a 12-tablet venue (numbers shaped on last week's live fleet). Every metric used is listed at the bottom with where it comes from — most we already collect.

Option B
The letter: the week in sentences

Written like an account manager's weekly email. Numbers appear inside sentences, never as bare tiles. Doubles as the body of the "Email report" message.

Harbor & Vine · week of 21–27 September

Hi Sam,

Your 12 tablets had a good week. They were ready to take orders for 97% of your opening hours — up from 92% the week before. The one dip was Friday dinner, when Table 6 lost its Wi-Fi from 6:20 until close.

Guests charged their phones on the tables 184 times (63 hours in all), most of it on Friday and Saturday between 7 and 9pm. Patio 1 and Table 2 were the most popular spots.

Your team is doing well with charging: tablets went back on the charger at around a third full on average, and only Table 4 ran flat — twice, on Saturday night and Sunday lunch. A top-up between services would fix that.

Two small things for this week: the charging cable on Bar 1 is wearing out (we'll send a new one), and Patio 2 got very hot on Wednesday and Thursday afternoon in direct sun — shading it or moving it in for those hours will keep it from slowing down.

— The AIO team · Full per-tablet details are on the second page.

Good atThe friendliest by far; reads like a person looked at their week. Context comes for free ("the one dip was Friday dinner"), and it is the natural email body — nobody has to open a PDF to get the point.
Bad atSentences have to be generated from rules, and bad generated prose is worse than a table. Hard to scan for one number, and it does not scale past a handful of issues — a bad week becomes a long, alarming letter.

Good atEach reader gets their page and can stop there: the GM reads one headline sentence and four lines, the person who fixes things gets every number we have today. No grade to dispute. Works as a two-page PDF and as an email (page 1 is the body, page 2 the attachment). Degrades well: with no problems the list just says so.
Bad atLess warm than B, less visual than C or D. Relies on nicknames like C, and on opening hours like D, for its two best lines — venues missing them fall back to serials and a 24-hour day.
My pick: F, with B's sentences as the email body. The report's job for a non-technical reader is "did it work while we were open, and what do I do" — F answers both on one page without asking them to read a table or trust a grade, and it keeps today's numbers intact on page 2 for our own team, so nothing we rely on is lost. Two things make every option better and are worth doing whatever you pick: name tablets by where they sit (the nickname field already exists — prompt for it at enrolment), and count only opening hours (set them for every venue; the report falls back to 24 hours without them). I would drop the wireless-charging drain %/min from the venue's page entirely — it is our engineering question, not theirs.
