Daily Report — shorter

The report already comes back structured: a status, a one-line headline, a few metrics, a list of issues (each with a detail and an action) and a list of things that look fine. The card then renders nearly all of it, plus a per-restaurant alert breakdown — which is how a "brief" ends up longer than the page it sits on.

Four ways to cut it. Nothing here needs a different model or a new prompt: each option is a decision about which of those fields the card shows, and when.

A · What is on the page today deployed

Headline, full prose, metric tiles, every issue with detail and action, the "looking fine" chips, and the per-restaurant alert table underneath.
Daily Report your fleet briefingupdated 12:04

The fleet is broadly stable, with two venues worth keeping an eye on after a week of mixed charging behaviour and one unit running hot through the afternoon service.

Across 36 units, charging coverage averaged 71% of the day, down four points on last week, with the decline concentrated at Sunset Blvd where three tablets spent most of the afternoon off their pads. Overnight battery peaks held at 94% fleet-wide, which suggests the pads themselves are working and the gap is handling rather than hardware.

Thermals were unremarkable other than AT070AABU00231, which peaked at 47°C during the lunch period and stayed above 44°C for just over an hour. Memory pressure remained well within limits everywhere, peaking at 61% on the busiest kiosk.

Good at Nothing is hidden, and the prose carries nuance a tile cannot.
Bad at It is the largest thing on the page and it repeats what the KPI strip, Sites widget and Alerts page already say. Long enough that the two real issues are read last.

B · One line, expandable my pick

Collapsed by default to the status dot, the headline, and a count. Everything else is one click away. The report stops competing with the page and becomes what it claims to be — a glance that tells you whether to read further.

Good at Respects the reader on a normal day — most days the answer is "nothing to do", and that should take one second to learn. Issues keep their action line, which is the only part anyone acts on.
Bad at The prose paragraphs disappear; whatever nuance was in them has to survive in the issue details or it is lost.

C · Two columns: what to do / what is fine

Keeps everything, drops the prose. Actions on the left, reassurance on the right, metrics as a strip. Same information, a third of the height.

Good at Scannable, and the "fine" column is genuinely useful — it says what was checked, so silence reads as checked rather than skipped.

D · Issues only, nothing when clean

The card renders only when there is something to act on. A good day shows a single quiet line in the header instead of a card.

Good at The strongest signal of all — a card appearing means something happened. Zero cost on quiet days.
Bad at People stop trusting what they cannot see; the one-line "what was checked" is doing a lot of work to prevent that. Also hides slow drifts that are not yet issues.

My pick: B, with D's quiet-day line. Collapsed to one sentence and a count, expanding to the issues with their actions — and on a day with no issues, the line reads "nothing to act on" and names what was checked, so brevity never reads as the report having failed. C is the better choice if you want the report to stay a fixed, readable block rather than something that changes size day to day; say the word and I will build that instead. All four drop the per-restaurant alert table — it duplicates the Alerts page, and it is the single biggest thing on the card today.
