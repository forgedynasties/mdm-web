The folded child rail — second round

Ticks are out. These six are different answers to the same 56px, and none of them is a letter: each is built from something the row actually has — its position, its count, its health, or whether it is the one you are on. Hover any mock to fold it, as the real thing does. For reference, what is on stage now is the tick column you did not like.

G · Numbered, and the numbers are real shortcuts my pick

Folded, the strip is 1–9 in the mono face, the current one accented. The number is not decoration: ⌘1…⌘9 jumps to that row, open or folded, which is the thing a rail of filters is actually for once you know the page. Good: teaches a faster way to use the dashboard simply by being visible; stable, countable, never ambiguous. Bad: a number means nothing on your first day — it is a power-user affordance that the first week spends ignoring. Caps out at nine rows; the rest are just dashes.

H · Weight bars — the fold carries the data

Each row folds to a bar as long as its share of the fleet: the venue with 6 devices is visibly bigger than the one with 3, and an empty group is a stub. Good: the only option here that is more informative folded than a label would be — you can see the shape of the fleet while reaching for the nav. Bad: it answers "how big" when you are usually asking "which one", and two venues of equal size look identical.

I · Health column

Folded, every row is just its status dot — green, amber, red, grey — so the strip becomes a column of fleet health that happens to be navigation. Good: the one piece of information you would interrupt yourself for is the one that survives the fold, and it reuses a vocabulary the whole dashboard already speaks. Bad: on a page whose rows have no health (Settings tabs, alert filters) it degrades to a column of identical grey dots.

J · Only where you are

Folded, the strip keeps one thing: the row you are on, as its swatch, with a quiet "4 more" under it. Everything else goes. Good: answers the only question the fold has to answer — am I still in the venue I filtered to? — and spends nothing on the rest. Bad: you cannot switch rows without opening it again, so the fold is purely a reminder, not a target.

K · Spine — it folds to 26px and says its name

Not a list at all: the rail collapses to a narrow spine carrying the rail's own name sideways and an accent mark where the current row sits. The content gains the 30px. Good: no fake list, no per-row cipher, and the one word it shows ("Collections", "Settings") is the thing initials were trying and failing to say. Bad: rows disappear entirely, so you lose the position cue, and rotated type is the one typographic trick used nowhere else here.

L · It rotates — vertical rail becomes a top strip

While the app rail is open the page's rail does not shrink, it moves: the same rows run along the top of the content as a strip, full names and all. Leave the app rail and it swings back down the side. Good: nothing is abbreviated, nothing is hidden, and the content keeps its full width because the strip costs 42px of height it mostly has to spare. Bad: the biggest motion of any option — two rails changing axis at once — and long rails (Settings' eight tabs) will scroll sideways.

Pick: G, with I's dot kept as a pip. A number is the only mark here that is unambiguous, countable and stable for every kind of rail — and making it a real shortcut turns a dead 56px into the fastest way to move around a page you use daily. L is the one I would build if you want "nothing is ever abbreviated" taken literally; K is the quietest; H is the most fun and the least useful.
