Alerts, the Overview, and what the MDM should do next

A review built on the live server's own data: every alert raised from 16 to 29 September (14 days), the devices' daily temperature and battery records, and 30 days of commands and dashboard activity. Part 1 is what the alerts are actually doing. Part 2 is how to fix them, with the effect measured against the same 14 days. Part 3 is what the Overview could add. Part 4 compares the MDM with what fleet-management tools are expected to do.

What the data says

  1. Four rules fire on normal operation. 116 of 130 slow charge at night alerts ended at 80% battery or more, where every lithium battery slows its charge on purpose. 66 of 69 overheating alerts were between 48 and 52 °C, which is where the T7 sits every day (the average daily peak is 41–48 °C; the healthd reading also jumps 3–4 °C on the charger). All 125 temperature elevated alerts are the 42–48 °C band the fleet normally lives in. All 163 battery high overnight alerts say a charging tablet reached 100% at night. That is 470 alerts (72%) that describe the hardware working as designed.
  2. One fault arrives as a pile. 80% of alerts fired while the same device already had two or more other types open. A tablet with a bad charger raises slow charge, charger flapping, battery low, then offline, each as its own critical or warning, instead of one "charging problem on AT070AABU00864".
  3. Critical does not mean "act now". 279 alerts were critical, and 169 of them fired between 22:00 and 07:00 Los Angeles time. Slow charge at night (130, critical) is the biggest source. The Teams channel takes every critical alert in real time and announces its resolve as well: up to ~40 messages a day, most of them overnight and most of them normal.
  4. Nobody works the list. In the audit log, alerts were cleared in bulk 37 times ("resolve all" 20, "clear all" 17) and handled individually 7 times. Median time to resolve is 6 hours for the battery alerts and 36 hours for offline. The list is being emptied, not read, which is what happens when most of it is noise.
  5. Crashes are raised per device, but they are per build. com.google.android.permissioncontroller crashed 75 times on 40 devices in 14 days. That is one firmware bug for the release team, not 40 device problems for the restaurant.
  6. Two rules double-count one event. offline (after 30 min) and offline during peak (after 5 min) both fire for the same outage.

Source: the live alerts table (16–29 Sep), device_daily_stats, device_events and audit_log, queried read-only on 30 Sep.

Part 2

Fixing the alerts: fewer, grouped, and routed by whether someone can act

Six changes. The first two were measured on the same 14 days: together they turn 649 alerts into 98 device problems, about 7 a day, and take overnight critical alerts from 169 to 21.

The last 14 days, replayed with the first two changes

The six changes

1 · Retune four rules

Stop alerting on normal behaviour

Slow charge at night: only below 80% battery, and warning, not critical. Overheating: 52 °C for the T7 (per hardware model, not one number for all), sustained for 10 minutes. Temperature elevated: remove; it becomes a heat trend on the device page. Battery high overnight: remove as an alert; if battery wear is the concern, it's a charge-limit policy plus a weekly battery-health line in the report.
Effect on the 14 days
649 alerts → 179. Nothing that needed a human is lost: the kept alerts are offline, battery below 20% in service, real overheating above 52 °C, crash, charger faults, dead wireless pad.
−72% volume
2 · One problem per device

Group alerts that overlap on the same device

Alerts open on the same device at the same time become one problem with a list of symptoms and a likely cause: slow charge + flapping + low battery = "charging hardware"; offline on every device in a restaurant = "restaurant network". The problem is what gets listed, counted and sent; the symptoms are inside it.
Effect on the 14 days
179 kept alerts → 98 problems (about 7 a day, across 28 placed devices).
One card per fault instead of four or five
3 · Critical means someone can act now

Severity follows opening hours

Critical only when service is affected while the restaurant is open: a restaurant or a whole device type down, POS or payment offline, battery about to die in service. The same problem at night is queued for a morning digest to the restaurant's owner and support, not a real-time ping.
Effect on the 14 days
Overnight critical alerts 169 → 21. Critical overall 279 → 97; grouping crashes by build (change 4) and making slow charge a warning take off another 35, leaving about 60 in 14 days, almost all of them devices offline or battery low while the restaurant is open.
Pings only when a person can do something
4 · Crashes grouped by build

A crash signature is a release problem

Crashes are grouped by app + error + build and raised once on the release ("v2.1.024: permissioncontroller crashes on 40 devices"), linked from the Releases page. A device only gets its own crash problem when it is in a crash loop or the crash is in our app during service.
Effect
21 critical per-device crash alerts → a handful of release issues, with counts that show whether a new build made things better or worse.
Goes to the firmware team, not the restaurant
5 · A list people can work

Assign, acknowledge with a reason, snooze

Every problem has an owner (a person or "restaurant"), acknowledge with an optional note, snooze until a time, and a resolve reason: fixed, false alarm, known issue, hardware replaced. "False alarm" counts per rule feed straight back into tuning, so the next noisy rule shows up in a number instead of in someone's patience.
Why
Today the only tools are one-by-one resolve and "clear all", and the data shows which one wins.
Replaces bulk-clearing with triage
6 · Teams gets problems, not alerts

One message per problem, updated in place

One message when a problem opens, updates to that message as symptoms are added, and "resolved" only if the problem paged someone. Night-time problems go into the morning digest. Per-restaurant routing so each restaurant's contact hears only about their own devices.
Effect
From up to ~40 messages a day to a few, each one worth reading.
A channel people keep notifications on for

Part 4

What the MDM should do: where it stands

What fleet-management tools for dedicated devices (Esper, SOTI MobiControl, Intune, Hexnode, Scalefusion) are generally expected to do, next to what this MDM does today, taken from the code. Most of the basics are here, and some go well beyond the norm (firmware OTA with A/B slots, live remote screen, per-device telemetry at this depth). The gaps are mostly around operating the fleet: triage, safe rollouts, per-restaurant settings, and account security.

Recommendation

What I would do, in order

  1. Fix the alerts first. Retune the four rules, group alerts into one problem per device, make critical mean "open and affected", send overnight problems as a morning digest, and let Teams post problems rather than alerts. The replay says this turns 46 alerts a day into about 7 problems, and it is mostly configuration plus a grouping step. The digest option already exists in the settings; it only needs a sender.
  2. Put problems and service uptime on the Overview (cards A and B), and add the inventory clean-up card (C). Together they answer "is anything broken, is every restaurant up while it's open, and is the fleet list honest?"
  3. Add "Collect logs" as a device and bulk action. It replaces the most common use of the shell by far, and it's safer than handing people a shell.
  4. Then the operating gaps: staged rollouts that halt on failures, per-restaurant policies, and two-factor login plus role-scoped API keys.

Pick which of these to do and I'll start with demos for the screens (the problem card, the Alerts page, the Overview cards) before building anything.
